Brief Biography

Steam locomotion operates on the principle of external combustion, converting thermal energy from burning fuel into mechanical kinetic energy through the expansion of steam. The process begins in the firebox, where the fuel (usually coal, wood, or oil) is burned to generate intense heat. This heat is then transferred through the walls of the boiler, which is a sealed vessel fillwed with water. This causes the water to boil and and turn into high-preasure steam. This steam rises into the steam dome, where it is collected and dried before beging piped to the engine component.

Steam pressure (typically 200-300 PSI) is regualted by the engineer using a throttle which controls the flow of steam into the cylinders. Inside each cylinder, the steam pushes a piston back and forth. This motion is controlled by a valve gear which controls the timing of allowing steam to enter one side of the piston and its exhaust from the other allowing for much more precise control. The pistons are double-acting, which means steam pushes the piston both forward and backwards, maximizing efficiency.

The reciprocating linear motion of the pistons is converted into rotation through several mechanical links. The piston shaft is connected a crosshead, which guides the rotation straight, and then to a connecting rod. This rod attatches a crankpin on the driving wheels, transforming the back and forth motion into rotation. Coupling rods link the driving wheels to other wheels on the locomotive, ensuring that power is distributed across all axles for traction. Finally, the exhaust steam released through a blast pipe up the smokestack. This creates a draft that pulls air through the firebox which is what produces the very iconic chugging sound steam engines make.
